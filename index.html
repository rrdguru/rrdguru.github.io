01 / Research agenda

From what people experience
to what they choose.

My research starts with a deceptively simple question: what does product quality mean to a customer, and what happens when that experience accumulates over time?

Quality is not simply a feature of a product. It is an experienced judgment, formed across encounters, expectations and outcomes.

My doctoral work developed and validated a scale for customers' experienced product quality. Subsequent work examines how quality perceptions connect to brand attachment, satisfaction, loyalty and willingness to pay. I am also developing research on sustainability, AI and quality, and on the gap between what consumers know and what they choose.

03 / Teaching philosophy

Less framework-reciting.
More thinking.

Across marketing, analytics and research methods, I want students to understand why a concept matters, when it is useful, and what it cannot tell them.

The goal is not to remember a tool. It is to know when to trust it, when to question it, and what to do next.

My classroom connects concepts to real decisions, evidence and consequences. Students should leave able to explain their reasoning, not merely reproduce a model.

More on teaching and feedback ↗
01

Begin with a meaningful question

Start from a decision, customer problem or research puzzle before introducing the framework.

02

Make reasoning visible

Use cases, demonstrations, data and structured discussion to show how conclusions are reached.

03

Build critical independence

Ask students to test assumptions, recognise uncertainty and identify where an answer may fail.

04

Use technology with purpose

AI and analytics should extend inquiry and judgment, not replace either one.
